**HISTORY OF PRESENT ILLNESS:**
This is a 56-year-old individual with a significant smoking history who presents for an initial medical oncology consultation to discuss a new diagnosis of non-small cell lung cancer. The patient was in their usual state of health until approximately three months prior to presentation, when they developed a persistent, non-productive cough and progressive shortness of breath with exertion. Over the past several weeks, these symptoms have been accompanied by right-sided chest wall pain and an unintentional weight loss of approximately 10-15 pounds. These concerns prompted an initial evaluation which led to diagnostic imaging. The patient remains fully ambulatory and capable of all self-care, though their activity is limited by dyspnea. **DIAGNOSTIC WORKUP:**
Initial workup began with a chest X-ray which revealed a large right-sided lung mass. A subsequent contrast-enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was performed, which characterized a large, spiculated mass in the right upper lobe with extensive mediastinal and contralateral hilar lymphadenopathy. There was no evidence of distant metastatic disease to the liver, adrenal glands, or other visceral organs.

A PET/CT was performed for staging, which demonstrated intense FDG-avidity in the primary right upper lobe mass and in multiple mediastinal, hilar, and supraclavicular lymph node stations, consistent with extensive locoregional disease. No distant hypermetabolic foci were identified to suggest metastatic spread.

A staging MRI of the brain was performed and was negative for any evidence of intracranial metastases.

To obtain a tissue diagnosis, the patient underwent bronchoscopy with endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS)-guided fine-needle aspiration of a mediastinal lymph node. Pathologic review of the specimen confirmed a diagnosis of poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma.

Based on the combination of a large primary tumor with extensive contralateral mediastinal and supraclavicular nodal involvement (N3) and no evidence of distant metastases (M0), the patient is staged as having Stage IIIB disease.
