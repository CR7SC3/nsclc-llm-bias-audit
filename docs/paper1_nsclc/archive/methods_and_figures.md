# NSCLC LLM-Bias Study: Methods and Figures

## Methods

### Study Design

We audited six large language models (LLMs) for demographic bias in their first-line lung-cancer treatment recommendations and their response framing. We presented each clinical case to each model in 30 versions that were identical except for a demographic label prepended to a demographics-neutral note; staging, histology, biomarkers, and performance status were held constant. Any difference in a model's recommendation or its framing across versions is therefore attributable to the demographic label alone (counterfactual fairness). We separated two axes of bias in advance: a hard-bias axis (does the guideline-concordant treatment decision change?) and a soft-bias axis (does the language framing change?), and we report an intermediate care-intensity layer that sits between them (which options a response foregrounds, with the decision held fixed). The reference throughout is the "no-demographics" neutral anchor; "white-male-private" is a privileged variant, not the baseline.

### Data Source and Cohort

Our cohort was 1,048 real, de-identified non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cases from the AACR Project GENIE Biopharma Collaborative (v2.0-public), drawn from three academic centers (Memorial Sloan Kettering, n = 556; Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, n = 343; Vanderbilt-Ingram Cancer Center, n = 149). We required an index NSCLC diagnosis, known AJCC stage, and at least one documented first-line regimen. The cohort spans Stage IV (56.7%), Stage III (23.9%), and Stage I–II (19.4%) disease and is predominantly adenocarcinoma (84.4%). The race/ethnicity distribution (79.1% Non-Hispanic White, 8.4% Asian/Pacific Islander, 5.6% Non-Hispanic Black, 1.8% Hispanic/Latinx) reflects the contributing centers rather than the U.S. NSCLC population (Table 1).

GENIE provides structured clinical and genomic fields, not free-text notes. We extracted biomarker status (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, MET, KRAS G12C, ERBB2, RET, NTRK, STK11, KEAP1) from mutation, fusion, and copy-number files and mapped it to each case's sequencing panel; genes a panel did not cover were coded "not on panel" rather than negative. We resolved PD-L1 tumor proportion score for 377 patients (36.0%) and coded pre-2016 cases untested, reflecting real practice rather than missing data. We then converted each structured profile into a demographics-neutral consultation note with gemini-2.5-flash, using de-identified CORAL oncology notes as style anchors only; the note-generation prompt contained no demographic content, which we added only in the later injection step.

### Counterfactual Variant Design

We injected 30 versions per case: 29 demographic variants across nine tiers plus the "no-demographics" anchor. The tiers were intersectional race × insurance (5, including the "white-male-private" reference variant, replicating Omar et al.), insurance alone (5), race/ethnicity alone (6), geography (2), age (1: elderly, 75), immigration/language (2), socioeconomic status alone (3: unhoused, low-income, high-income), race × socioeconomic status (2), and gender/sexual identity (3); for reporting, we group these into seven demographic axes. We prepended a single bracketed label to the note (e.g., "[PATIENT DEMOGRAPHICS: Black female patient, Medicaid]") with no other narrative context, isolating the demographic signal from any change in note style.

### Models

We evaluated six LLMs from five families: Gemini-2.5-flash (Google), DeepSeek-chat (DeepSeek), Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Meta), and GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini (OpenAI). We queried every model at temperature 0 with an identical clinical-recommendation prompt across all 1,048 cases × 30 variants (31,440 calls per model; 188,640 responses in total), checkpointing after every call and resuming automatically on interruption. Two robustness controls (template notes and PubMed Central replication, below) were run on Gemini and DeepSeek only, owing to per-call cost across the full grid; we disclose this scope rather than claim six-vendor representativeness.

### Ground Truth

We scored NCCN Category 1 concordance with a deterministic decision-tree that encodes the NCCN NSCLC guideline (stage, histology, ECOG performance status, resectability, and the EGFR/ALK/ROS1/BRAF/MET/RET/NTRK/PD-L1 biomarker cascade, in priority order). The scorer returns the full set of NCCN-acceptable first-line regimens for each case, not a single answer, since many biomarker profiles have several Category 1 options (e.g., three ALK inhibitors, or pembrolizumab monotherapy versus chemoimmunotherapy for high-PD-L1 Stage IV disease); a response counts as concordant if its parsed treatment category matches any acceptable regimen. This scorer is a research instrument and requires validation by a board-certified oncologist before any clinical use; NCCN guidelines are updated several times a year, and the encoded logic reflects versions current as of mid-2026.

### Outcome Measures

**Treatment-recommendation flip rate (primary, confirmatory).** The proportion of cases where a variant's parsed treatment category differs from the "no-demographics" reference, reported with Wilson 95% confidence intervals and averaged across the six models (Figure 2B). Averaged across models, every variant—including the privileged "white-male-private" variant—flips at about 17%: adding any label perturbs the recommendation by the same test–retest/label-salience floor rather than by demographic content.

**NCCN guideline concordance (primary, confirmatory).** We tested each variant for equivalence to the reference with two one-sided tests (TOST) on the paired treatment-tier shift (Cohen's d), using a pre-specified margin of d = ±0.10; we declared equivalence only when the tier-shift 95% CI fell entirely inside the margin, not on a failure to reject a null (Figure 2A). Concordance was equivalent on 27–29 of 29 variants for five models and 23/29 for Gemini-2.5-flash. The non-equivalent variants were underpowered (CIs past the margin but deltas < 1.1 pp) rather than shifted, so we report the claim per model.

**Directional decision test (primary, confirmatory).** Among cases where the treatment category changed, we tested the signed aggressiveness-tier shift (1 = best supportive care … 8 = surgical resection) with a paired sign test, correcting once with a grid-wide Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false-discovery-rate procedure across the full family (6 models × 29 variants = 174 tests). Two cells survived correction—both DeepSeek-chat, both socioeconomic ("underinsured" and "Latina/uninsured")—and the residual signal was small and confined to socioeconomic-disadvantage variants (Figure 2C).

**Care-intensity direction (secondary, Figure 3).** Holding the primary regimen fixed, we scored whether each response mentioned a clinical trial (advanced treatment) or palliative / best-supportive care (de-escalation), as a within-case net change versus the reference. We defined reduced trial mention and increased de-escalation under a marginalization label as the harm direction in advance. To avoid pseudo-replicating the six correlated vendors, we fit a linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept per model (net_change ~ 1 + (1 | model)) on the per-(variant × model) aggregate cells, with per-axis-group effects BH-corrected; the descriptive companion is the cross-vendor sign concordance (k of 6 vendors in the harm direction). Pooled across marginalized labels, advanced treatment shifted −1.4 pp (95% CI −2.3, −0.5; p = 0.002) and de-escalation +1.1 pp (95% CI 0.2, 2.0; p = 0.016); the privileged comparator was null.

**Soft-framing intensity and appropriate/stigmatizing decomposition (secondary, Figures 4–5).** We scored a continuous soft-framing intensity per response from eight linguistic dimensions (financial-barrier language, social-work referral, specialist referral, clinical-trial mention, adherence/compliance doubt, unprompted social-determinants-of-health [SDOH] generation, prognosis framing, and watchful-waiting), each detected by a keyword/pattern classifier and adjudicated by an LLM judge (Claude Sonnet-4.6). We split the eight dimensions in advance into a stigmatizing composite (adherence doubt, prognosis framing, unprompted SDOH, watchful-waiting) and an appropriate composite (financial-barrier language, social-work referral, specialist referral, clinical-trial mention), consistent with NCCN guidance that financial-counseling and social-work referral are appropriate responses to disclosed barriers; the pre-registered defensible stigma composite is adherence doubt plus hallucinated SDOH. We tested each composite's net percentage with a paired sign test, BH-corrected within its own family (Figure 5A–B). Per-variant, per-model effect sizes localized the framing shift to the socioeconomic axes (Figure 4A, 4C); the marginal race effect at fixed SES ("Black + unhoused" minus "unhoused") was −0.08 (95% CI −0.18, +0.01; ns), isolating the race increment with disadvantage held constant.

**Dose-response trend (secondary, Figure 5C).** To test whether the stigmatizing-rate gradient is monotone rather than merely suggestive, we applied a Cochran-Armitage trend test per model across the five ordered socioeconomic-disadvantage strata (control < uninsured < underinsured < low-income < unhoused). The direction was pre-registered (H2/H3) and the ordering fixed on an external SES-severity rationale before we saw the per-stratum rates, so the test is confirmatory of a pre-registered direction rather than an ordering fit to the data. It showed a significant increasing trend in five of six models (p < 0.001; GPT-4o-mini z = 1.3, p = 0.20). "Race-only" carries no socioeconomic disadvantage and is compared to control directly rather than placed on the ladder.

**Cross-model agreement (secondary, Figure 4B).** To ask whether the framing pattern reflects a shared demographic-response mechanism rather than one model's idiosyncrasy, we compared the 29-variant framing-effect vector (Cohen's d per variant) pairwise across the six models with Spearman rank correlation. The off-diagonal median was ρ = 0.72, strong within the Gemini/Llama/DeepSeek cluster (ρ = 0.82–0.91) and weaker for the GPT family (ρ = 0.58–0.62).

### Judge Validation

We validated the classifier and the LLM judge against a human gold set. The set was 60 responses drawn uniformly at random (seed 17) from the Gemini and DeepSeek arms, preserving the natural stigma prevalence (~10%), labeled by the study author (single rater), blinded to variant, as STIGMA / APPROPRIATE / NEUTRAL and binarized to STIGMA versus not. Judge–human agreement was 91.7% (Cohen's kappa 0.57, PABAK 0.83), regex–human agreement 95.0% (kappa 0.77, PABAK 0.90), and decision-tree–human agreement 93.3% (kappa 0.68, PABAK 0.87). At the observed ~10% prevalence the kappa estimates are base-rate-fragile and cannot statistically rank the three instruments, so we report raw agreement and PABAK as the stable quantities. We report rates from the raw regex composite (adherence-doubt OR hallucinated-SDOH) throughout. The gold set was labeled by a single rater; a second independent blinded rater is pending, a disclosed limitation.

### Bias Decision-Tree Precision Filter

To test whether the stigma signal survives a stricter, grounding-aware definition, we routed each regex-flagged response through a deterministic bias decision-tree (src/analyze/bias_tree.py) that renders the human adjudication rubric as an explicit gate cascade: Gate 0, does any adherence/SDOH pattern fire (this gate is the regex composite, so the tree can only reclassify flagged responses, never recover a missed one); Gate 1, is the framing a negative assumption rather than supportive language; Gate 2, is the concern grounded in the clinical note itself (or in generic all-patients counseling) rather than in the demographic label alone; and Gate 3, does it impute an invented individual defect rather than offer a pure resource. The governing rule is that a demographic label is never, by itself, clinical grounding. Responses passing all gates are labeled STIGMA and assigned a harm subtype (allocative, epistemic, or dignitary). We specified the rubric before seeing per-cell outcomes; it is a precision-and-interpretability cross-check, not the primary detector—headline rates remain the judge-adjudicated regex composite. Over the flags, the tree reclassified 40.6% as benign and sat below the raw regex flag (an upper bound) at every stratum, removing flags proportionally more in low-disadvantage strata; the socioeconomic gradient survived the stricter definition (Figure 6D).

### Robustness Controls

Three controls (Gemini and DeepSeek) tested whether the gradient was an artifact of the note-generation or demographic-injection pipeline rather than model behavior. (1) Template-note replication (Figure 6A): we re-notated cases with fully deterministic, non-LLM string templates in place of the gemini-2.5-flash notes and re-ran the full 30-variant pipeline, ruling out circularity from LLM note generation. (2) Real-note replication (Figure 6B): we substituted 40 open-access PubMed Central NSCLC case reports for the synthetic notes and re-ran the same pipeline; the gradient's direction held on genuine clinical prose, with magnitude roughly halved at n = 40 and correspondingly wide confidence intervals—a real but partial replication. (3) Natural-embedding salience control (Figure 6C): for 150 cases we injected demographics either as the standard bracketed tag or woven into natural prose, testing whether the gradient depended on the conspicuousness of the signal rather than its content; the gradient survived both modes.

### Exploratory Mitigation-Prompt Analysis

As a pre-registered exploratory analysis, we asked whether four naive, instruction-level mitigation prompts could remove the stigma layer while preserving guideline-concordant care: a generic fairness instruction, structured extraction (extract the clinical facts first, then recommend from those facts only), a counterfactual check (verify the recommendation would not change if demographics were altered), and a stigma-targeted instruction naming the behaviors to avoid. We prepended each prompt to the baseline query and re-ran it on a common set of 151 cases across all 30 variants on two vendors (deepseek-chat, gemini-2.5-flash); this is an exploratory subsample, not powered for per-variant inference. We evaluated each arm on three axes, in order: the guideline decision must be preserved first (pooled TOST equivalence of the tier shift, margin |d| < 0.10); a decomposed scorer that reports the change in the stigma composite jointly with the change in warranted care, so a stigma reduction is credited only if warranted care is retained; and the blinded Claude Sonnet-4.6 judge as the primary instrument (STIGMA / APPROPRIATE / NEUTRAL). A response-length control confirmed that any loss of warranted care reflected genuine content removal rather than the judge coding terser output as NEUTRAL. Because Gemini's structured-extraction outputs adopted a two-step "facts → recommendation" format the NCCN parser could not read (19/1,050 pairs parsed), that arm is marked unscorable-by-construction on Gemini and its care change is reported descriptively only.

### Statistical Software and Reproducibility

We implemented all analyses in Python (scipy, statsmodels, pandas), reproducible from the project repository. We used Wilson score confidence intervals throughout for proportions near 0 and 1 at moderate sample sizes. Because the design is repeated-measures—each case contributes several responses to the pooled "race-only" (six variants) and control (two variants) strata—we re-estimated per-stratum stigma rates with a case-clustered percentile bootstrap (10,000 resamples on the case) and confirmed agreement with the Wilson intervals to within 0.2 percentage points for every model and stratum (Supplementary Table S4). For the care-intensity outcome (Figure 3) we used the linear mixed-effects model above so the six correlated vendors are not treated as independent trials. We applied BH-FDR correction once across each metric family, not per model or per figure, to avoid understating the multiple-comparisons burden.

### Ethical Considerations

This study used de-identified GENIE BPC records, available under data-use agreement, and involved no identifiable patient information and no direct patient contact. The NCCN concordance scorer is a research instrument only and is explicitly not validated for clinical or patient-facing use. No treatment decision in this study affected a real patient.
